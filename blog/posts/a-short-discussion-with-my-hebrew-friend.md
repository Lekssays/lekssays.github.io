## Introduction

Since the first era of the social sciences with Ibn Khaldun in the 14th century, culture has been a central topic, because it involves the study of groups of people. As Hofstede G. defines it, "It is the collective programming of the mind which distinguishes the members of one group or category of people from another. It is what makes a human being belong to a group" (Hofstede 7).

According to an article published by the University of Washington, babies begin to learn language while still in the womb. They listen to what their mothers say in order to build the linguistic system that will determine their mother tongue after birth. They also start learning the other aspects of their parents' culture from their first hours of life. They begin the adventure of discovering what is around them by observing how people behave, and from there they build their own experiences based on what their parents tell them, what they see in their families, and what they observe in the wider environment. In short, human beings are what they have learned from their surroundings.

One of the important aspects that varies from one culture to another is accepting the other. The other, in this case, may mean a person, a group, or a culture that is different from yours.

## The Interview

In this interview, I compared how Islamic culture differs from Jewish culture. I looked at aspects such as dress code, marriage, and way of life, which can involve people of a different religion, gender, or ethnicity. I asked a Jewish person how she sees these things.

I wanted to experience cultural relativism, that is, how people react toward a different environment and on what basis they judge it to be superior or inferior to their own culture. I also wanted to test my ability to deal with a person who is literally different from me in ethnicity, religion, and gender. In addition, I wanted to discover a culture that has long been transmitted to us as a monster we should neither talk to nor talk about, even though Jews have been part of Moroccan culture for a very long time.

## Dress Code

The dress code in Jewish culture has some strict aspects. For instance, it is prohibited to wear a garment made of both wool and linen, and it is prohibited for a man to wear a woman's garment and vice versa. However, priestly dress is the main topic discussed in detail in the Torah, because it serves as a reminder to the Jew to follow what is written there.

I found an interesting fact about the dress code in Jewish culture: it reflects religious identification, social status, and emotional state. For instance, a married Jewish woman must cover her hair to show outsiders that she is no longer single. Overall, there is a law that governs covering the body, but it is stricter with women than with men. Another interesting fact is that women cannot wear pants, as they are strictly prohibited. Instead, they can wear long skirts, but it is prohibited to wear tights, even if the legs are visible.

All these aspects depend on which Jewish group, Orthodox, Conservative, or Reform, the Jew belongs to. However, they are now influenced by other cultures. Globalization has brought change to Jewish culture by altering some traditional aspects of the dress code. For instance, women have started wearing pants, which was prohibited in the traditional dress code. On this point, the dress code of Islamic culture is stricter, but it does not reflect personal or social status.

## Marriage

Marriage in Jewish culture has some special conditions that surprised me when I compared them with the Islamic conditions. Jewish culture does not allow intermarriage in any direction. This point led us to talk about the religious aspects of both faiths.

In Judaism, the mother is the central part of the family, because if she is Jewish, her children will be Jews. So if a Jew marries a woman from another religion, their children will not necessarily be Jews; they might follow their mother's religion. This fact explains why intermarriage is not allowed. In addition, Judaism believes in the concept of soul mates, which means that another part of the Jew exists somewhere else, sharing the same religion.

Concerning divorce, the man can divorce a woman for any reason or for no reason at all, but in traditional Jewish law the woman is not allowed to start the divorce process. Overall, divorce is considered an unfortunate necessity, which is a point in common between Islamic and Jewish culture.

## Way of Life

Finally, ways of life differ between my culture and Jewish culture on many levels. For instance, some Jewish groups believe that they should not stay in one place for a lifetime, because God sent them to spread around the Earth. On this point, I discovered something new: some Jewish groups are against making Israel a state, because building it contradicts the mission God assigned to them on Earth.

I also learned that extremism has no religion, because there are extremist groups who claim to represent Judaism and who kill innocents. I tried to compare them with the extremist groups who claim to represent Islam and who kill innocents around the globe, and I found that these extremist groups should not be linked to a religion, because they represent only themselves, not the religion.

## Conclusion

To sum up, I learned new things by comparing my culture to Jewish culture:

- Communication is the key. We should not pass judgment before we really talk and interact with the other.
- There are always good people and bad people in every religion, and we should not link the bad actions of people who think they belong to a religion to the religion itself. We should distinguish between a religion and the people who believe in it.
- Extremism is mainly a problem of a lack of communication and an inability to accept difference.
