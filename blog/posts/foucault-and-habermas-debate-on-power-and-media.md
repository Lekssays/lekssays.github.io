## Introduction

This paper sets out to explain the importance of power and its effect on media, along with the key points of the Foucault/Habermas debate on power. This debate never actually took place in real life. The only interaction between Habermas and Foucault occurred at the Collège de France, when Foucault invited Habermas to a conference in 1983. Rather, the followers of Habermas and Foucault constructed this debate between genealogy and power analytics as ways to explain the behavior of power in society, set against the communicative rationality and discourse ethics that Habermas proposed to explain that same behavior.

In what follows, we discuss Foucault's ideas on power and their relation to media. We also address the critiques he received for viewing power from that angle. Finally, we turn to Habermas, who criticized Foucault, and to his contribution to the concept of the public sphere and its relation to media.

Power is a critical variable that determines how media will operate in a given society:

- If power is treated as an absolute tool that cannot be questioned, media become an instrument in the hands of the regime, used to manipulate people's voices, to show what the regime wants seen, and to hide what might lead people to question it.
- If, on the other hand, power is treated as a tool to protect people's voices by giving them the chance to speak and to discuss the issues that concern their environment, media become a free and important element in the growth of any nation.

When we question power, we should turn to the French philosopher Michel Foucault, who spent years trying to explain how power works. He addressed several important concepts, such as discipline, punishment, knowledge, and discourse.

## Foucault on Power

Power is a visible and productive relation that is exercised throughout the social body. It is not something strictly tied to the State. Foucault stated that power "reaches into the very grain of individuals, touches their bodies and inserts itself into their actions and attitudes, their discourses, learning processes and everyday lives" (Foucault, 1980). He sought to show that power is not possessed by a certain elite, nor is it tied exclusively to the actions of certain individuals. This also reflects Foucault's interest in people's freedom and in how people behave under various circumstances. Power, then, is everywhere, and it operates at every level of the social body.

Foucault's definition of power opposed the way the Marxist and Liberal theories defined it. They limited it to the bourgeoisie, which is wrong according to Foucault, who considers power a cross-level relation. He also links the existence of resistance to the existence of power: wherever there is power, there is resistance. Moreover, when we discuss Foucault and power, we should mention his Power/Knowledge theory, which defines a correlation between power and knowledge, since knowledge is a form of power:

> Knowledge linked to power, not only assumes the authority of 'the truth' but has the power to make itself true. All knowledge, once applied in the real world, has effects, and in that sense at least, 'becomes true.' Knowledge, once used to regulate the conduct of others, entails constraint, regulation and the disciplining of practice. Thus, 'there is no power relation without the correlative constitution of a field of knowledge, nor any knowledge that does not presuppose and constitute at the same time, power relations (Foucault, 1977).

Foucault did not interpret power as a negative thing. He saw it from a different perspective: as an effect on people's actions and as a relationship between individuals. He questioned the difference between individuals' actions in private and their actions when power is exercised on them or by them. To explain power from his angle, he defined three types of power: sovereign power, disciplinary power, and pastoral power.

### Sovereign power

Sovereign power is usually related to obedience to the rules and to the people in charge of managing society, and it is defined by blood-right or blood-conquest (Foucault, 2007).

### Disciplinary power

Disciplinary power refers to the fact that discipline is the mechanism that manages the behavior of individuals within society. This can be achieved by organizing the space, time, and daily lives of individuals. Foucault argues that disciplinary power gave birth to prisons, since they are a type of spatial organization (Foucault, 1977). Discipline is implemented by setting up complex surveillance systems.

To explain this relationship, Foucault used the concept of the panopticon, introduced by Jeremy Bentham. The panopticon is a circular prison that facilitates the surveillance process. It allowed Foucault to explain the Power/Knowledge relationship and the actions of individuals in a disciplinary context. To understand the change in people's behavior, he related the different actions of the observer and the observed (Foucault, 1977). Surveillance leads to the acceptance of the rules by the individuals being watched, because they always act as if someone is watching them, even when this assumption may be wrong. On the other hand, Foucault also focused on the observer's side to explain the Power/Knowledge relationship. The observer's behavior changes depending on how the individuals being watched act. So the observer's behavior depends on how much knowledge he or she gains from repeated observations. The observer becomes powerful in the eyes of the individuals because the observer knows more about them (Foucault, 1977).

At this point, we can conclude that surveillance is not necessarily related to technology, even though the term is often used in that exclusive context. Surveillance and the panopticon can explain how individuals deal with media and technology today. Individuals shape their actions on social media to fit what the people monitoring the media want. They do not act as they wish, because they always assume that someone is tracking and monitoring them. At this point, individuals do not pay explicitly for social media services; instead, privacy has become the new currency. Individuals pay for services in an implicit manner, which requires them to act in a way that does not necessarily reflect how they behave in real life. They tend to deliver the perfect image they want to share with the world, rather than how they actually are. This concept applies to media. Surveillance is the key element in disciplinary power, because it establishes control over individuals and media. Power, however, is not discipline, because discipline is a way to exercise power, not a power in itself.

### Pastoral power

Pastoral power focuses on governing individuals by placing them in political groups that can be managed. Foucault also addressed knowledge and power, arguing that they cannot be separated: power is an exercise of knowledge, and knowledge is a function of power. In other words, knowledge is a power in itself. This relationship explains why Foucault considers power a productive relation. When social institutions use knowledge to produce weapons, they merely represent their power in these fields, because knowledge is just a form of power.

## The Debate with Habermas

Foucault's main critiques arose from his approach to understanding power. He considered historical analysis the main key to understanding how power behaves and is exercised in a given society, because it shows how humanity has moved from traditionalism to modernity. Foucault said of Habermas: "I am interested in what Habermas is doing. I know that he does not agree with what I say. I am a little more in agreement with him." (Foucault, 1983). This shows that the intellectual debate intersects at some point: both consider knowledge a key element in understanding the relationship between power, society, and individuals' actions.

On the other hand, Habermas said: "I can only relate what impressed me [about Foucault]; the tension… between the almost serene scientific reserve of the scholar striving for abjectivity on the one hand, and, on the other, the political vitality of the vulnerable, subjectivity excitable, morally sensitive intellectual." (Habermas, 1984). Habermas's main criticism of Foucault's approach to power came from the fact that Foucault derived his ideas from knowledge, which is ambiguous according to Habermas (Habermas, 2014). He also criticized Foucault for rejecting enlightenment and modernity. Still, both Habermas and Foucault relate power to knowledge and vice versa, though they see them from different angles: Foucault relates reason to power, while Habermas defines different types of reason.

## The Public Sphere and Media

Habermas claimed that the public sphere holds a power that can shape political opinion, which can in turn legitimate the regime in any society (Habermas, 2014). The public sphere is not necessarily a physical place, because it can exist virtually. It provides a platform for individuals to share opinions and ideas that bear on their society as a whole. Habermas gave a historical analysis of the public sphere, tracing it from a liberal era to a media-centred era. As a result, the role of media changed from facilitating debate in the public sphere to constructing, shaping, and influencing public opinion through the topics it discusses (Habermas, 2014).

At this point, Foucault discussed the issue from a different angle. He invoked the concept of surveillance, which is used to control society because individuals act differently when they know they are being watched. Surveillance is also applied to media in order to limit its freedom and shape the topics discussed so that they serve the system's agenda. So the flow of information that once moved through the public sphere has been manipulated by media, which transform citizens into consumers who absorb the content media provide. As a result, the builders of public opinion have become mere spectators.

## Conclusion

To sum up, Michel Foucault defined power as a productive relation that is not possessed but exercised. Power, according to Foucault, can exist at all social levels, which counters the Marxist view that limits it to the bourgeoisie alone. In this paper, we attempted to relate the concept of surveillance, as defined through the experience of the panopticon, which explains the change in human behavior when surveillance is exercised on a person, on one side, and the change in the behavior of the people who control or monitor the tools, on the other. The more knowledge people have about each other, the more power they gain over each other. This relationship holds in the context of social media and the internet, because individuals tend to act as if they are being watched rather than as they are. Foucault also raised the concept of surveillance as it is applied to media and citizens to manipulate public opinion and thereby secure political stability.

On the other hand, Habermas criticized Foucault for linking power to knowledge, holding instead that knowledge comes in different types. In his writing, he introduced the important concept of the public sphere and showed how it evolved from its liberal state to a media-centred one. This transition changed the essential role of the public sphere, from gathering opinions about general decisions that concern the individuals who form public opinion, to a manipulated opinion concentrated on what media provide.
