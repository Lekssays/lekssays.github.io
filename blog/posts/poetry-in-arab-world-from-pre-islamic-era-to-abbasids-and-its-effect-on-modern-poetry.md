## Introduction

Poetry has long been a major tool for expressing the state of a community. Arab poetry played a great role in establishing, maintaining, and destroying political systems in the Middle East and North Africa region from pre-Islamic times to the present day. It was used to promote religion, sultans, and governments because it had a magical effect on people. They listened to it because of its beauty, and so it stuck easily in their minds.

Arab poetry, or “Chi’ir,” is entirely different from novels, articles, or other forms of Arabic literature because it is so advanced in terms of language. Arab poets, or “Chu’ara,” played with words, choosing the powerful ones that could affect people quickly once they grasped the meaning. Because it cannot be interpreted easily, poetry was used to ruin the reputation of a sultan without the poet being caught quickly. It carries many deep meanings that serve several purposes beyond the ones already discussed.

Poetry is still used in education today and is part of the primary school program. However, schools do not teach pre-Islamic poetry, because it is very different from the Arabic taught in classrooms. Instead, students learn the two main types of poetry: the free and the traditional.

In the pre-Islamic era, there was only a single type of poetry: the traditional. It had strict, hard rules to follow, which is why it was so advanced. Each poem had to have a fixed number of “paragraphs” and a unique song at the end of each line called “Quaf’ia,” the part that allows people to sing the poem. In addition, each poem had to belong to a meter, or “Baher.” Arabic poetry has 16 such meters, as they are technically named.

Free poetry, on the other hand, appeared in the 20th century. It served several purposes, such as nationalism, love, and the revolution of the Arab peoples:

- Nizar Qabbani used free poetry to write about love in an artistic way.
- Mahmoud Darwich used it to address the Palestinian-Israeli conflict, conveying his opinion as a Palestinian poet through the power of his words.
- One of Abu Al-Qasim Shabi’s poems serves as a national anthem of Tunisia. It is a revolutionary poem that encourages people to change their destiny, because they have the power to do so.

Poetry was also used to motivate people to push the colonial powers out of their motherlands. There, language did not matter, because in the North Africa region, especially in Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, and Libya, the Amazigh poem was a major tool for reaching people who did not understand Arabic. In fact, they were the soldiers of the Moroccan army at that time.

## The Pre-Islamic Era and the “Mu’allaqat”

Pre-Islamic poets laid the foundations of Arabic poetry. The culture of this era was oral, and what is discussed in books now is only what the authors of the post-Islamic era transmitted to us. As a result, it is somewhat biased, because some practices of the pre-Islamic era were prohibited later on. For instance, describing the bodies of girls was allowed in the pre-Islamic era but prohibited after Islam.

This period was known as the dark age of the Arab peoples. In fact, it was not as Muslim authors described it. It is known as “Jahilia,” which means the age of ignorance. However, that is not really the case, because the level of language and the power of the “Quraish” leaders’ speeches were advanced compared to those of today’s leaders.

The “Mu’allaqat” are the long poems of the pre-Islamic era, or “Jahilia.” They are written in a pure, artistic language to describe the lives of the Arab people in the Arabian Peninsula at that time. They were hung in the Kaaba because of their importance, which is the origin of their name “Mu’allaqat,” since the word means, in Arabic, something that is hung. They can be divided into seven types, each known by a poet:

- Harith Ibn Halza
- Imru Al-Qays
- Zuhair Ibn Abi-Salama
- Tarafa Ibn Al-Abd
- Amr Ibn Kulthum
- Antara Ibn Shadad
- Labid Ibn Abi-Rabiaa

Each “Mu’alaqa” addresses a different topic depending on the interest of each poet.

### Harith Ibn Halza

Harith Ibn Halza is one of the great poets of the pre-Islamic era. He wrote about his tribe, “Baker Ibn Wael.” He composed “Mua’lasa Fakhriya,” which promoted his tribe; in other words, he wrote the pride poem of the “Mua’laqat.” He was so proud of his tribe that the Arab people used his name to describe a proud person in daily life, telling such a person that he was prouder than Harith Ibn Halza. This “Mua’laqa” addressed the daily life of his tribe and urged people to prepare for war.

### Imru Al-Qays

Imru Al-Qays is one of the famous poets of the pre-Islamic era. He wrote about love. In his “Mu’allaqa,” he described his love for Leila, a girl from his tribe. His story with Leila resembles that of Romeo and Juliet, and the story of Qays and Layla is a famous love story in Arabic culture. He was known for describing the bodies of girls in his poems, which was not accepted after Islam, so the authors who wrote about him described some of his poems as obscene. He has inspired Arab poets such as Nizar Qabbani.

### Zuhair Ibn Abi-Salama

Zuhair Ibn Abi-Salama wrote his poems over one month and prepared them over one year, a period that means “Hawl” in Arabic. His poems were called “Hawliyat” because of their long preparation. They were respectful, in the sense that they were accepted after Islam. He eulogized people for what they were known for. Omar Ibn Al-Khattab named him the poet of poets because of his beautiful style. He wrote about what he knew, so his poems were genuine.

### Tarafa Ibn Al-Abd

Tarafa Ibn Al-Abd is considered one of the best poets of the pre-Islamic era. His poems are about ethics, humanity, life, and the traditions of the Arab peoples at that time. He tried to show the bright side of the Arab peoples by describing their daily lives and how they helped each other. In addition, he described the tools and industrial development they were achieving. Historically speaking, his “Mu’allaqa” helped researchers understand the lifestyle of the Arab people in the pre-Islamic era and compare it to life after Islam, in order to determine what changes Islam brought to Arab culture.

### Amr Ibn Kulthum

Amr Ibn Kulthum is another great poet of the pre-Islamic era. His poems were about motivating people to take part in the wars against his tribe. He was proud of his tribe, which is why he wrote about it in a prestigious way. He thanked the brave people who belonged to the same tribe and described them as models for others.

### Antara Ibn Shaddad

Antara Ibn Shaddad is one of the famous poets of the pre-Islamic era, because he wrote about his love story and its relationship to the life of slavery. Antara lived a different life from the other poets, because his father was his owner according to the rules of slavery. He wrote about his father’s wife, because she was the source of his problems. She told his father that Antara was harassing her, just to see him beaten every time. The interesting part of these stories is that she would also ask his father to stop the beating whenever she felt that he had suffered enough.

Antara’s poems viewed the Arab culture of that time from another angle, because he wrote about the miserable lives of slaves. He showed the world that slaves can love as well. He is known for his poems about Abla, his lover and cousin. He loved her and wrote long poems describing his deep feelings toward her. She was one of the most beautiful girls in his tribe, and he met her secretly, because meeting a girl at that time was prohibited. He went to his uncle to ask to marry Abla, but his uncle refused completely, because Antara was a slave and black. He wrote in his poems about racism and how slaves did not have the right to marry. The exact end of the story is not known: some authors say he did not marry her, while others claim that he married her even though her father refused. Their story has inspired many writers, because it is seen as a model love story today, especially after the formalization of human rights.

### Labid Ibn Rabia

Labid Ibn Rabia is the last poet of the “Mu’allaqat,” because he lived a long time after Islam, making him one of the poets who experienced both the pre-Islamic and post-Islamic eras. He wrote about the hospitality of the Arab people, wine, love, and pride in belonging to his tribe. After he became a Muslim, his poetry changed, because he no longer wrote about what Islam prohibited, such as the obscene description of girls’ bodies and wine. Labid is one of the poets through whom we can sense the change that Islam brought to the Arab peoples in the Arabian Peninsula at that time. Some of the topics that were dominant in the pre-Islamic era were prohibited afterward, so Islam changed the topics that poets could cover in their poems.

## The Transition Between “Jahilia” and Islam

The era between “Jahilia” and Islam was critical, because poetry was seen as an unethical act for a Muslim, given that the poets of “Jahilia” were known for drinking wine and for obscene descriptions of what Islam prohibited. It was therefore a pivotal time, one that shifted the topics toward pride in belonging to a tribe, motivation to take part in wars, and the promotion of Islam and the Prophet.

### Hassan Ibn Thabit

Hassan Ibn Thabit was one of the famous poets of that era, because his poems were all about the Prophet Muhammad. Before Islam, Medina was divided into two tribes, “Aws” and “Khazraj.” Hassan Ibn Thabit was the poet of “Khazraj,” and Qaiss Ibn Khatim was the poet of “Aouss.” He defended his tribe and wrote about wine as a major element of the Arab culture at that time. After Hassan joined Islam, he completely changed the topic of his poems to the life of the Prophet Muhammad. He wrote his poems to defend Islam and urge people to join it. He treated this as an intellectual war against those who attacked Islam at that time, considering the defense of Islam through poetry to be like defending it with weapons.

## The Umayyad State and New Forms of Poetry

This transitional era gave birth to a new type of poetry. After the conflict between Muawiya and Ali, the source of the division between Shia and Sunni, poets took both sides, communicating their political opinions through poems.

In the Umayyad State, a new type of literature was born: Islamic literature, another variation of the “Jahilia” literature, in the sense that it respected the new rules Islam set for Muslims. In addition, another type of poetry appeared, called “Naqa’id,” which involved writing a poem that promotes one poet and ruins the reputation of others.

- **Al Akhtal** was one of the great poets of the Umayyad state, because he promoted the Umayyad caliphs and attacked the other poets who did not agree with them, using his strong words.
- **Jarir** is another famous poet of this era. He was at war with Al Akhtal, as they exchanged harmful poems that showed the Arab community their powerful mastery of the language and its beauty in conveying messages without any offensive or disrespectful words.

The other type dominant at that time was praise poetry. It served political purposes, and because the Arabic of these poets was extraordinary, they were special and important in every era of Arab history. **Al-Tarmah Ibn Al-Hakim** was one of the famous poets who wrote this kind of poetry.

A further new type was “Zuhd” poetry, which the Sufi movement later used to express itself. **Hassan Al-Basri**, the founder of the Sufi movement, used this kind of poetry to show people the rules of Islam and urge them to join it. He also used it in “Da’awa” to draw people to religion and to encourage them to forget this life and work for the life after death. It appeared because of the effect of Islam on the Arab people, who were looking for a way to get closer to Allah. “Zuhd” poets were different, because they were not social: they believed that the real life is the one after death, and that poets should contribute to spreading Islam and defending it as well.

## The Abbasids

After the Umayyad state was destroyed, a new Islamic state appeared: the Abbasids. They were more open-minded, so the topics of their poetry were richer. In addition, new words were added to Arabic through the translation of books from other languages into Arabic. The poetry also changed because of the new lifestyle of the Arab people at that time.

The translation of books influenced the topics of poetry and changed its language. It was a revolution in the history of poetry, because poets began to use non-Arabic words, moving away from the traditional, pure Arabic vocabulary used in the pre-Islamic era and the Umayyad state. This change produced open-minded poets such as Abu Firas Al-Hamadani, Abu Tayeb Al-Mutanabbi, and Abu Al-Alae Al-Maarri.

### Abu Firas Al-Hamadani

Abu Firas Al-Hamadani was a great poet of the Abbasid era, because he was the cousin of Saif-Al-Dawla Al Hamadani, the governor of Aleppo at that time. The governor discussed the situation of the state with the Ulama and poets, meeting with Al Mutanabbi, Al Farabi, and Abu Firas. Abu Firas became Amir of Manbij, but he faced political problems with his ministers, and as a result he was imprisoned for more than seven years. His poems were therefore about patience and his hard experience in jail. They were full of negative emotions, because he was suffering and conveying what he felt in prison.

### Abu Tayeb Al-Mutanabbi

Abu Tayeb Al-Mutanabbi played a major political role in Saif-Al-Dawla’s state and was one of its decision makers. He was proud of himself and his race, and he wrote about himself in his poems. He was a great dreamer, which is why his friends were amirs and caliphs. He praised them to gain money and authority, as he wanted to become a wali in the Abbasid state, and he wrote about Saif-Al-Dawla. Because of this, he lived a life of luxury.

However, other poets wanted to create conflict between him and Saif-Al-Dawla through their poems. They succeeded, and he left Aleppo. He traveled to Egypt and wrote poems against Saif Al-Dawla, asking him what had led him to trust the other poets. The same story repeated itself with Kafur, the governor of the Ikhshidid dynasty.

He also wrote about the failure of some aspects of the intellectual revolution of the Abbasid dynasty, drawing on his own experience and failures. He described how he had lived a life of luxury and ended up living a miserable one. This was the main cause of the critical change in the topics of his poems: he stopped promoting caliphs and began writing about his miserable experience. His poems played a major role in illustrating the change between the Umayyad and Abbasid dynasties.

After the Mongols burned the books of Baghdad and defeated the Abbasid dynasty, the Arab world entered a new era that was less productive and creative than what it had experienced under the Abbasids. It was known as the age of decadence, or “Al Inhitat.”

The Abbasids played an important role in making the Arab people more open-minded by exposing them to other cultures. However, as Al Mutanabbi stated in his last poems, the Arab world was facing a critical failure, and so the level of poetry declined. The important productions of that era were “Al Muqaddimah” by Ibn Khaldoune and “Lisan Al-Arab” by Ibn Mandour.

## The Arab Renaissance (“Al-Nahda”) and Modern Poetry

The Arab world inherited some Western concepts, such as political parties, which played a major role in creating the Arab renaissance movement, or “Al-Nahda.” As a result, poetry and literature in general recovered from the critical state they had been in. The topics of poems were enriched again, because some Arab poets were living in Western countries and integrated Western culture into their poetry. In addition, the poems fought against the concept of taboos.

A new type of poetry was born, building on the foundation that the Abbasid poets had provided. They had introduced the concept of free poetry, which differed from the traditional poetry inherited from the pre-Islamic era. However, at that time, they had changed only some aspects of the poem’s form. In “Al-Nahda,” poets transformed free poetry completely, even changing the meter that a poem should have. It served several purposes, reflecting the development of the Arab world:

- Some poets chose it as a way to defend their nations, such as **Mahmoud Darwish**, who used it to share Palestinian thoughts on the Palestine-Israel conflict with the entire world.
- It was also used in the national anthems of several countries. For instance, the poem “Al Qadar” by **Abu Al-Kassim Al-Shabi** is the official anthem of Tunisia today. It was easy to understand compared to the “Mu’allaqat,” which helped this new style promote nationalism.
- **Nizar Qabbani** used free poetry to express his emotions toward women, breaking the taboo of writing about women in a sexual way.
- **Ahmed Shawki** was a great poet of that era. He wrote many poetic stories for children that influenced the famous French poet “Jean de La Fontaine.”

## Conclusion

To sum up, poetry is a powerful tool that can influence people easily. It was used by most Arab rulers to control their peoples and to promote themselves. It was refined to share political opinions and promote nationalism after the introduction of free poetry in the Abbasid era. It also helped to record the history of the Arab people before Islam and to define their way of life.
