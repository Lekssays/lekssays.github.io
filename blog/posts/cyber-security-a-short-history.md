## Cyber Security in the Headlines

Cyber security has become one of the hot topics discussed across the media today. Over the past five years, countless articles have covered hackers being arrested or banks being attacked with nothing more than viruses and malware.

- **Petya:** The most recent attack, Petya, is a well-implemented ransomware that struck many countries around the globe (Hern & Solon, 2017). Ransomware is a class of malicious programs that lock an individual's data and demand payment to unlock it.
- **WannaCry:** Two weeks earlier, the WannaCry ransomware targeted computers running the Windows operating system. It hit more than one hundred countries worldwide, freezing metro operations in several German cities and disrupting many hospitals in England.

## The Origins of Computing

Historically, the theory of computing was born out of Great Britain's need to decrypt the ciphers of the Nazis. The Nazis sent their messages in an encrypted format that changed every twelve hours, so British scientists had only twelve hours to decrypt a message that had billions of possible permutations because of the Enigma encryption. This proved impossible to do by hand: even if they could test one permutation per second, they would never finish decrypting the message in time (Copeland, 2005).

## Alan Turing and the Bombe

A British scientist named Alan Turing came up with an idea that shortened World War II by fourteen years. It was based on cryptography, one of the fundamental fields in cyber security. Turing noticed that the messages always began with a name, which let him narrow the search dramatically. He then built a machine called "the Bombe" to decrypt the messages computationally, setting up a model grounded in cryptanalysis and mathematics.

He reached his goal of decrypting Enigma in 1943, helping the Allies evade German forces during the Battle of the Atlantic. Breaking Enigma was one of the challenges that drove the rise of cyber security as we know it today. In this sense, computer science was invented to serve humanity and to help end the war, regardless of the political meaning of stopping World War II (Copeland, 2005).
