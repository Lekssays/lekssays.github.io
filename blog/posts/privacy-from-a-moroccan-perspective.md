## The HackingTeam Leak

In 2015, an Italian company that sells hacking software such as Trojans and malware was breached by an anonymous team, which leaked about four hundred gigabytes of data. Surprisingly, the leaked data revealed how much money Morocco had spent buying spyware from HackingTeam. The Moroccan intelligence services spent around 3 million euros on spyware to control the flow of information between Moroccans and the rest of the world, on the one hand, and among Moroccans themselves, on the other (Blum-Dumontet, 2015).

## How Much Do Moroccans Value Privacy?

To understand how important privacy is for the Moroccan citizen, consider a comparison between two major events from the same year:

- The incident in one of the Moroccan stadiums hosting the 2015 FIFA Club World Cup, where a match was stopped because of rain.
- The massive leak of HackingTeam data, which directly affects the privacy of Moroccan citizens.

The media impact of the first event was enormous: people engaged intensely with the stopped match while almost entirely ignoring the HackingTeam leak, except for the Moroccans interested in cyber security. Culturally speaking, we can say that privacy is not that important to Moroccans. In our social behavior, we tend to share everything we have, and hiding our personal information does not really matter to us. As a result, we do not give our private data the value it deserves. Moroccans tend to underestimate it, even though it can be critical and very useful to others who get hold of it.

## Privacy Is the New Currency

Individuals enjoy free services online. However, the term "free" does not mean these services are literally free, because users pay with something else. With ordinary services, you buy something online by paying for it in currency. With the new "free" services such as Facebook, Twitter, and Pinterest, individuals enjoy the service without thinking about the price they are paying. They do not care about their privacy. Big companies sell their data to marketers, so the price of these free services is privacy. Privacy has become the new currency: you pay with your privacy and with how much information you share with these platforms (Bennett & Raab, 2006).

## The Limits of Security and Consent

Legally speaking, these platforms state clearly in their long privacy policies that data becomes part of their databases the moment the individual clicks the "agree" button. They write lengthy privacy policies to discourage people from reading them, but users have no real choice: they must either agree or leave the platform. Even so, it is sometimes worthwhile to read privacy policies and terms of use, because they give a clear idea of how a service handles privacy and private information.

At this level, the discussion is only about the risk of data being used by a third party, such as Black Hats, the hackers who steal, destroy, and sell private information. Security is an illusion, because there is no absolute security. Governments have legal access to the private information of any service hosted in their territory, so individuals are not safe from being spied on by their own governments (Bennett & Raab, 2006).
